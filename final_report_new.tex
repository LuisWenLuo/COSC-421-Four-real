\section{Centrality and Delay Propagation}
\subsection{Introduction to Metrics}

In order to determine the correlation between structural centrality of the major Canadian airports and their role, if any, in delay propagation, several centrality metrics and delay metrics were compared. For analyzing structural centrality:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
    \item Degree (in, out, and total)
    \item Strength (in, out, total, and log)
    \item Betweenness Centrality
    \item Closeness Centrality
    \item PageRank
    \item Undirected Eigenvector Centrality
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
For analyzing delay propagation outcomes:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
    \item n\_pairs (number of origin–destination pairs that involve the airport in question)
    \item n\_severe\_prev (number of severe ``predecessor'' events that arrived at this airport)
    \item n\_propagated (number of events which propagate from this airport)
    \item propagation\_rate (proportion of severe predecessors that propagate)
    \item amplification (index of whether delays are generally amplified or dampened by this airport)
    \item rho\_spearman (Spearman correlation between previous and subsequent delays with this airport)
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Centrality and Delay Relationships}

Since the analysis focused on only Canada's five major airports, correlation magnitudes are useful more as a descriptive measure. However, they still reveal important patterns.

\subsubsection{Centrality vs. Delay Volume}

For volume metrics (n\_pairs, n\_severe\_prev, n\_propagated), correlations with centrality are very high, as demonstrated by the Pearson correlation coefficient.

\[
r_{XY}
=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}
     {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}
      \sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
\]

\subsubsection{Centrality and Propagation}

\paragraph{Propagation Frequency}
While it is clear there is a correlation between the centrality of an airport and how many delays it experiences, different results arise when considering the question of whether central airports are more likely to propagate or amplify delays. In this regard, data is more inconsistent.

Correlations between centrality measures and propagation\_rate are inconsistent. Thus, it can reasonably be concluded that among major airports, structural centrality alone is not a major determining factor in the frequency of inbound delay propagation. It is likely that operational factors within the airport are responsible for moderation of these delay events.

More central hubs show weaker links, meaning the severity of a single predecessor event is not a strong predictor of a future delay.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

With the statistical analysis performed on the five major Canadian airports, we can generalize a clear relationship between structural centrality and delay propagation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Centrality and delay volumes tend to be highly correlated. Airports with high strength and centrality (i.e., closeness, PageRank, eigenvector centrality, and betweenness) see many more severe incoming delay events and also propagate more delay events. Central airports act as the carriers of delay through a network in aggregate.

    \item Centrality alone is not a predictor of individual delay propagation chance. The propagation rate (fraction of severe events that propagate) varies weakly with centrality. Very central airports such as YYZ can have lower propagation rates than less central airports, which contradicts expected logic. This may imply that non-structural factors are greater determiners of propagation rates.

    \item Centrality has a stronger relationship with amplification and dampening behaviour. Most central airports have a close to neutral amplification, meaning they neither reduce nor worsen delays. The least central airport, YEG, displays strong dampening, with YUL being an outlier and showing some positive amplification. This relationship suggests that the position of an airport in the network may change how airports affect the magnitude of a delay, not just propagation.

    \item Delay coupling is negatively associated with centrality. The Spearman correlation demonstrated that the relationship between upstream and downstream delays is highest at the least central airport and lowest at the more central hubs. High centrality appears to blunt the effects of a single upstream event, even despite higher total delay volume.
\end{enumerate}

Overall, structural centrality in the Canadian air transit network strongly determines the overall delay volume of an airport and how it propagates that delay. However, it is only weakly correlated with the chance that a single severe delay event propagates downstream to other airports. Similarly, centrality is only weakly associated with the strength of the link between specific delay events from one airport to subsequent delay events at another. This suggests that, while centrality plays an important role in the overall volume and frequency of delays at a given airport, the underlying mechanics behind whether a given flight delay worsens or improves are more complex than simply being a product of the airport's geographical location.
